Plain language first, the numbers underneath

Hyperscalers, explained

How the companies that rent out the world's computing power make money, what the AI build-out costs, who pays for it, and what has to be true for it to pay off.

The short answer. The revenue is real, growing fast and, for the biggest players, backed by years of signed contracts. The bill is growing faster, and more of it now arrives as depreciation (the cost of chips wearing out) and interest. The winners would be the companies whose revenue from each gigawatt of data centers stays ahead of both. On today's numbers that favors the three cloud giants, which have large, profitable older businesses underneath them, and the companies that sell them chips and power equipment. The risk sits mostly with the GPU clouds and builders that borrowed to grow.

By Wilson Wu, an operator who studies AI engineering and screens small businesses to buy. Figures as of 7 November 2026.

Part 1: Situation

What the industry screen found

Out of 31 US industries I compared, one finished first on both profit margin (the share of each dollar of sales left over as profit) and growth: hyperscale data centers. They kept 29.9% of sales as profit, the highest of the 31, and grew 25.1% a year over 10 years after inflation, from about $29B to about $272B a year, according to IBISWorld's US industry report "Hyperscale Data Center Services" (updated 30 March 2026).

I'm an operator who studies AI engineering and screens small businesses to buy. To compare many industries the same way, I used the US industry reports from IBISWorld, a firm that publishes research on industries, and sorted 31 of them with four questions. Which earn the most? Which grow fastest? Which are steadiest? Which are shrinking? The numbers here are headline figures from IBISWorld's 2026 US industry reports, rounded.

Each answer is a bucket, and each bucket taught me one plain thing.

  • Most profitable. Six of the 31 kept 18% or more of sales as profit, including hyperscale data centers (29.9%), occupational health and safety services (26.7%), security software (24.9%) and insurance brokers (20.6%). One trap: IBISWorld's free "highest margin" ranking shows value added, which includes wages, not profit, so I used the profit figures inside each report. The lesson: a margin is an average across every firm in an industry, so it describes the industry, not any one company in it.
  • Fastest growing. Hyperscale data centers (25.1% a year), 3D printing services (18.3%) and security software (14.3%) lead, all measured over 10 years after inflation. The lesson: fast growth tends to fade. IBISWorld forecasts hyperscale data centers at 12.2% a year through 2032, about half the past rate.
  • Steadiest. Janitorial services, IT consulting and insurance brokers had no down year from 2012 to 2026. The lesson: steady is not recession-proof. In IBISWorld's long history, plumbers' revenue fell 20.8% from peak to trough between 2007 and 2011, and electricians' fell 19.9%.
  • Declining. Directory publishing is at 12.0% of its 2007 peak, newsprint at 15.7% of its 2008 peak and commercial printing at 52.9% of its 2007 peak. The lesson: a structural decline is not a bad year. Solar panel installation fell 10.4% in 2026, yet IBISWorld forecasts it to grow about 2.9% a year from here. Some falls recover. Others do not.

The weak spot

The screen ranks industries on paper. It told me hyperscale data centers earn well and grow fast. It could not tell me how a hyperscaler makes its money, what the AI build-out costs, or who carries the risk if it goes wrong. A ranking cannot answer those, and they are the questions that decide whether a number like 29.9% lasts.

That gap is why I studied the industry. The rest of this page is what I learned, with sources I could check. I'm not an expert, and I would rather show my work than sound sure.

The simple version. Imagine a guidebook that ranks restaurants by stars and crowds. It shows which ones are popular and well reviewed. It does not show what the kitchen costs, who owns the building, or what happens if the bank wants its loan back. The screen was the guidebook. The rest of this page is a visit to the kitchen.

Part 2: Task and action

The question, and how I answered it

The question: will the AI build-out pay, and who carries the risk? To keep from wandering, I split it with an issue tree, a way of breaking a big question into parts that do not overlap and together cover the whole. Five questions came out:

  1. Demand and revenue drivers: is the demand real, and what moves revenue?
  2. Costs and cost drivers: what does it cost to serve?
  3. Financing: who pays for the build, and with what money?
  4. Competition and who keeps the profit: where does the profit end up?

  5. Scenarios and what has to be true: what must happen from here for the build-out to pay?

  6. Filings. I read the latest 10-Q (a company's quarterly report to the US regulator, the SEC), 10-K (its annual report) and earnings release for 11 public companies: Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, Oracle, CoreWeave, Nebius, NVIDIA, Equinix, Digital Realty and Vertiv. Amazon's 10-Q and earnings release show the style. Quarterly figures are unaudited: outside accountants have not signed off on them as they do for annual accounts.

  7. Checks. I checked every figure I pulled against the filing text. Of 339 quotes, 337 sat on the exact line I cited, one sat elsewhere in the same filing, and I did not use the one I could not find. Quarterly capex (money spent on buildings and chips that last for years) was checked two ways: the SEC's machine-readable XBRL data against the earnings release.
  8. News. Full articles from The Wall Street Journal, The New York Times, the Financial Times and The Economist (the Times and The Economist through a university library).
  9. Sentiment. Themes from X, Hacker News and StockTwits. Reddit could not be read directly, so I do not quote it.
  10. Industry figures. Synergy Research, Dell'Oro, Cushman & Wakefield, JLL and the US Energy Information Administration, each checked against the saved source page where the site allowed it.
  11. A model. An illustrative campus of 1 GW (a gigawatt, a billion watts of power), to see what one site might earn and cost.
  12. Frameworks. Issue tree, pyramid principle (answer first, then the support), Porter's five forces (a checklist for sizing up competition), 7 Powers (seven kinds of lasting advantage), scenario planning (several futures, not one guess) and TAM/SAM/SOM (the whole market, the part you can serve, the part you might win).

The simple version. Judging whether a restaurant chain's new kitchens will pay works the same way. Ask if diners queue, what the kitchens cost, who lent the money, who the rivals are, and what must go right. Then check the claims against the receipts, not the menu.

How the business works

What a hyperscaler is

A hyperscaler is a company that runs data centers at a size few others can match and rents out the computing power inside them. Three giants lead: Amazon Web Services (AWS), Microsoft Azure and Google Cloud. Synergy Research Group's Q3 2026 release puts their shares of cloud infrastructure at 26%, 19% and 16%, or 61% together (Synergy). Meta also builds huge data centers, but mostly to run its own apps. Oracle and the neoclouds, newer companies such as CoreWeave and Nebius built to rent out computing for AI, sell capacity too, much of it to AI labs.

The easiest picture is renting instead of buying.

The simple machine

Think of a stack of layers, each needed by the one above it:

  1. Land and power. A site, plus a grid connection that delivers electricity around the clock.
  2. Buildings and cooling. Halls, backup power and cooling systems that carry the heat away.
  3. Chips and servers. Computers full of GPUs (graphics processing units, chips that do huge numbers of sums at once and do most of AI's heavy work).
  4. Network and storage. The cables, switches and disks that connect machines and keep data.
  5. Software. The services customers actually use, from basic virtual computers to ready-made AI tools.

The business is to build all five once, then rent the result out many times over.

Different companies earn money at different layers: utilities and turbine makers at the bottom, data center landlords such as Equinix and Digital Realty, chip makers such as NVIDIA and TSMC, and the clouds on top.

Who rents it, and why

  • A streaming service at peak hours. Millions watch in the evening and few at 4 a.m., so it rents extra capacity for the rush and hands it back.
  • A bank's apps. The apps must stay up and stay fast, so the bank rents space across several sites instead of building them all.
  • A startup training a model. Training can need thousands of GPUs for weeks. Buying them first would drain the startup's cash.
  • An AI lab serving a chatbot. Every answer takes computing, and the more people ask, the more capacity the lab needs.

The thread is the same: needs rise and fall, and owning is expensive.

Five ways to buy

  1. On demand. Pay by the hour, no promises. This is the most flexible and the highest price. For AI chips the unit is the GPU-hour: one GPU rented for one hour.
  2. Commit for a discount. Promise to spend a set amount for one or three years. AWS Savings Plans offer discounts of up to 72% against on-demand prices (AWS).
  3. Spare capacity. AWS Spot sells unused machines at up to 90% off, but AWS can take them back at short notice (AWS). It suits jobs that can pause.
  4. Multi-year contracts for whole data centers. A customer signs for years of capacity. Revenue that is signed but not yet delivered is called backlog, and formally remaining performance obligations. Microsoft reported $683B at 30 September 2026 in its 10-Q (unaudited). By my sum, the four biggest (Microsoft, Oracle, Amazon and Alphabet) hold about $2.49T. Later sections return to this.
  5. Per-token pricing for AI. A token is a small chunk of text, roughly a word or part of one. Amazon Bedrock lists Mistral Large 3 at $0.50 per 1M input tokens and $1.50 per 1M output tokens (Amazon Bedrock).

Why scale wins

Three advantages help the giants, and none is guaranteed:

  • Keeping machines busy. An idle machine earns nothing. Utilization, the share of capacity actually in use, can rise when many customers with different peaks share one pool.
  • Buying power. Huge orders for chips, equipment and electricity may bring better terms and earlier delivery than a small buyer gets.
  • Custom chips. Google designs its own TPU chips and Amazon its Trainium chips, so each can tune hardware to its own work.

Scale can also make buildings more efficient. Google reports a fleet-wide PUE (total power drawn divided by power that reaches the computers) of 1.09, against an industry average of 1.54 (Google).

Training and inference

Training is teaching a model by running it over enormous amounts of data, which can take thousands of chips for weeks. Inference is using the finished model to answer a question. One answer is small, but it happens millions of times. Training is writing the textbook. Inference is every student who looks something up. Where the rent comes from, and how fast each grows, is part of what this page asks.

The simple version. A hyperscaler is a very large landlord for computing power. Instead of buying computers that sit idle most of the day, a company rents what it needs by the hour, like taking a taxi instead of owning a car. The landlord's job is to keep every room rented.

For engineers and investors: GPU-hour prices, on demand versus a contract

The median on-demand list price for an NVIDIA H100 was $4.20 per GPU-hour on 7 October 2026, across 16 providers (Uvik GPU price index). SemiAnalysis's one-year contract index for the same chip stood at $2.35 in March 2026, up from $1.70 in October 2025, as cited by Uvik. By my arithmetic the contract price is about 44% below on demand, though the dates differ. For the newer B200, the median on-demand price was $8.19 (9 providers). These are list and index prices, not what any one customer pays.

Part 3: Result

Where the money comes from

Part 3 answers the five questions from the issue tree in order: revenue, costs, the build-out and who pays for it, who keeps the profit, and what happens next. Each section starts with its answer. Company figures come from each company's latest quarterly filing and are unaudited.

The answer: the demand is real, much of it is signed years in advance, and a large part of it rests on a handful of AI labs. For now, the companies' own numbers suggest the limit on revenue is how fast new capacity can be switched on, more than how many customers want it.

The market for renting computing power, which the industry calls cloud infrastructure services, brought in $167.7B in the third quarter of 2026, up 55% on a year earlier, and $558B over the last 12 months, according to Synergy Research Group. Three companies, Amazon, Microsoft and Google, hold 61% of it.

What moves that revenue? A consultant would draw a driver tree: break the total into the few things that multiply together to make it.

  1. Capacity online. How much computing power is switched on, measured in gigawatts (GW) of electricity it can draw. CoreWeave had 1.5 GW running at the end of June and 3.7 GW under contract. The gap is capacity still being built and connected, which is why, for now, supply more than demand sets the pace.
  2. How busy the chips are. A chip that sits idle earns nothing. The giants spread many customers with different peaks across one fleet, which helps keep machines busy.
  3. Price per unit. The median on-demand price for an NVIDIA H100, the workhorse AI chip, was $4.20 an hour in October across 16 providers, and $8.19 for the newer B200. Commit for a year and the H100 costs about $2.35 an hour, on a SemiAnalysis index for March 2026. That contract price had risen from $1.70 in October 2025, which suggests demand was outrunning supply even for older chips.
  4. Mix. Raw chip time is the cheapest thing a cloud sells. Databases, storage, security and ready-made AI models sit on top of it and earn more from the same gigawatt. That is likely a big part of why the giants earn far higher margins than clouds that rent mainly GPUs.

The last branch of the tree is the one investors watch most: backlog, the value of signed contracts not yet delivered. Filings call it remaining performance obligations.

The four largest backlogs add up to about $2.49T: Microsoft $683B, Oracle $664B, Amazon $578B and Alphabet $567.7B. Divided by current yearly revenue (the whole company for Microsoft and Oracle; AWS and Google Cloud for Amazon and Alphabet, where nearly all their backlog sits), that is about 1.9 years at Microsoft, 3.1 at Amazon, 4.6 at Alphabet and 8.6 at Oracle. The GPU clouds stretch further: CoreWeave's $103.7B is about ten years of its current revenue, and Nebius's $37.5B about 16. Timing matters too: Microsoft expects about 30% of its backlog to become revenue within 12 months, Oracle 13%, and Alphabet just over 55% within 24 months.

The catch is who signed. Microsoft recorded $10.0B of revenue from its commercial arrangements with OpenAI in a single quarter, according to a related-party note in its 10-Q. CoreWeave's largest customer was 36% of its revenue in the second quarter, and its largest a year earlier had been 71%. Anthropic, in the filing for its planned stock market listing, details about $895.4B of computing and infrastructure commitments through 2045, The Wall Street Journal reports. A backlog is only as strong as the customers behind it, and a large part of this one rests on a few AI labs.

The simple version. Picture a landlord who has signed tenants for an apartment tower that is still going up. The leases are real money, but the rent arrives only as each floor is finished, and two or three tenants have signed for a big share of the building.

For engineers and investors: reading a backlog
  • Remaining performance obligations are contracted revenue not yet recognized. Definitions differ: Amazon counts contracts longer than one year, mainly AWS, with a weighted-average remaining life of 6.1 years; Alphabet reports a revenue backlog, $560.8B of it Google Cloud; Microsoft's commercial backlog rose 73% in a year.
  • "Years of revenue" here is backlog divided by the latest quarter's revenue times four. It measures visibility, not a forecast.
  • Backlog becomes revenue only when capacity is delivered. That makes power, buildings and chip supply the main limit on near-term revenue, and it makes backlog quality (who the customer is, and whether it can pay) the real risk.

Where the money goes

The answer: AI has changed the shape of the cost base. A software company spends mostly on people. An AI cloud spends mostly on machines that wear out, so its biggest cost tends to be depreciation, the yearly write-down of chips, servers and buildings. Electricity is a smaller bill than most people expect, and one of the tightest constraints.

To make this concrete I built an illustrative model of one AI campus with 1 GW of computing load: about 600,000 of NVIDIA's newest GPUs in about 8,333 racks. It uses public inputs and it is a sketch, not a forecast. The code is in the repository linked at the bottom of the page.

The build costs about $52.6B. The building, power and cooling systems come to $17.6B, using Cushman & Wakefield's average of $17.6M per megawatt for new US and Canadian data centers before chips. Chips, servers and networking add about $35B, in line with Jensen Huang's estimate, reported in September 2026, that about $35B of a gigawatt's cost goes to NVIDIA systems. The GPU racks alone come to about $25B at analyst estimates of $3M per rack. Huang's reported all-in figure is $50B to $60B per gigawatt.

The yearly bill:

  • Depreciation: about $6.7B a year, spreading the chips over six years ($5.8B) and the building over 20 ($0.9B).
  • Electricity: about $0.8B a year. The campus uses about 8.4 terawatt-hours, priced at $96.40 per megawatt-hour, the US average industrial price in August 2026.
  • Running costs (staff, maintenance, network, insurance): assumed at 12% of revenue, about $1.0B at today's GPU-cloud prices.

Depreciation is about eight times the power bill. Power is a bottleneck, not the biggest bill: the scarce thing is a grid connection, not the electricity that flows through it. Several signs point to scarcity. The capacity auction run by PJM, a grid operator in the eastern US, cleared at its price cap for the third auction in a row in December 2025, at $333.44 per megawatt-day for the 12 months from June 2027. GE Vernova's backlog and reserved production slots for gas turbines reached 116 GW at the end of June 2026. US industrial electricity cost 9.64 cents per kilowatt-hour in August, up from 8.98 a year earlier.

Efficiency is the engineer's lever. Google's fleet runs at a power usage effectiveness (PUE) of 1.09, against an industry average of 1.54, so less of each megawatt is lost to cooling and power conversion before it reaches a chip. The model assumes 1.2.

The biggest lever is an accounting choice. How fast a company depreciates its chips depends on their useful life, the number of years it expects them to earn their keep, and companies give different answers.

In the illustration, cutting the chips' useful life from six years to four adds about $2.9B of expense a year. At three years, even a busy, well-priced campus would lose money. Here is what the companies chose, from their latest annual or quarterly reports:

  • Alphabet, Oracle and CoreWeave: six years (CoreWeave moved up from five in 2023).
  • Amazon: five to six years. In 2025 it shortened some servers from six years to five because AI technology moves faster, which added $1.4B of depreciation that year.
  • Meta: 5.5 years from 2025, which cut its 2025 depreciation by $2.92B.
  • Nebius: five years from 2026, up from four.
  • Microsoft: two to six years.

Two camps argue about this online. One says the lives are too long and flatter profits. The other points to the rising one-year rental price of the older H100 as proof that old chips still earn their keep. Both read the same facts; the next two years of rental prices should help settle it.

More depreciation is likely on the way. Over the last 12 months these companies spent from 2.7 times (Amazon) to 8 times (Oracle) as much on new assets as they recorded in depreciation. As those assets go into service, the expense should keep climbing. In the latest quarter compared with a year earlier (unaudited), Microsoft's depreciation of property and equipment rose from $7.1B to $11.1B, Meta's depreciation and amortization from $5.0B to $7.2B, and Oracle's depreciation from $1.4B to $3.2B.

The simple version. Buy a delivery van for $60,000 that will be worn out in six years, and $10,000 of its value disappears every year, whether you drive it or not. Accountants call that depreciation. In the illustrative campus, the vans are the chips and the building, and their yearly wear costs about eight times the electricity that runs them.

For engineers and investors: how the campus model works
  • Energy: 1,000 MW of computing load x PUE 1.2 x 80% average load x 8,760 hours = 8.41 TWh a year. At $96.4 per MWh that is $0.81B.
  • Depreciation: $35.0B of chips and servers over 6 years ($5.83B) plus $17.6B of building over 20 years ($0.88B) = $6.71B a year.
  • GPUs: 1,000 MW divided by 120 kW per 72-GPU rack (an NVIDIA GB200 NVL72) = 8,333 racks, about 600,000 GPUs.
  • Calibration: at CoreWeave's current revenue per gigawatt the model's operating margin (operating profit as a share of revenue) is -3.3%, against CoreWeave's reported -1.9%. In the busy case it is 36.9%, against AWS's reported 38.7%. Close enough to trust the shape, not the decimals.
  • Limits: it is a year-one view before tax. It ignores construction time, price declines as chips age, and resale value. The code is model/hyperscaler_model.py in the repository.

The build-out, and who pays for it

The answer: at most of these companies the AI build now costs more than their operations bring in, so the gap is being filled with debt, leases and other people's money. Over the last 12 months Amazon, Oracle and CoreWeave each spent more on new assets than their operations brought in. In the latest quarter, so did Alphabet and Meta.

Amazon, Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta and Oracle put $230.4B into property and equipment in their latest quarter (unaudited), more than double the $105.8B of a year earlier. Amazon alone spent $77.3B in three months. Amazon, Microsoft, Google and Meta are on track to spend more than $720B this year on data centers and other capital projects, about triple two years earlier, The New York Times reports. Worldwide, Dell'Oro Group expects data center capex to pass $1T in 2026 and $3T a year by 2030.

Measured against revenue over the last 12 months, Oracle spent 105% of its revenue on capex, Meta 43%, Microsoft 39%, Alphabet 34% and Amazon 27%. CoreWeave spent 271%. Measured against the cash their operations generate, Amazon spent 128%, Oracle 161% and CoreWeave 298%.

Free cash flow is the cash left after paying for new assets. The latest figures, using each company's own definition where it has one:

  • Amazon: -$42.4B over the last 12 months.
  • Alphabet: operations brought in $42.3B in the third quarter and capex took $51.4B, a gap of about $9.1B.
  • Meta: -$2.1B in the third quarter.
  • Oracle: -$5.4B in its latest quarter.
  • Microsoft, the exception: about +$18.6B in the quarter ($58.0B from operations less $39.4B of capex). Even there, an analyst quoted by the Financial Times argues that much of that cash comes from financing choices such as leases, stock-based pay and slower payments to suppliers, rather than core profit.

Three things stand out:

  1. At CoreWeave's current revenue per gigawatt, about $8.2B a year, the campus would roughly break even before interest (an operating loss of about $0.3B) and would need 8.2 years of cash profit to pay back its cost. Breakeven before interest would be about $8.6B a year.
  2. At $14.7B a year (600,000 GPUs rented at $3.50 an hour, busy 80% of the time) it would earn about 37%, close to AWS, and pay back in 4.3 years.
  3. Financing decides who can ride out a slow year. Funded 70% with debt at 10%, interest would be about $3.7B a year, turning the base case from about -$0.3B into about -$4.0B and lifting breakeven to about $12.7B. Funded mostly with cash and cheap bonds (30% debt at 5%), interest would be about $0.8B.

Where the money comes from now. For most of these companies internal cash is no longer enough, so the build is moving onto borrowed money and other people's balance sheets.

  • Bonds. Amazon's long-term debt went from $65.6B at the end of 2025 to $173.1B nine months later; it raised $115.1B of long-term debt in that time. Alphabet's went from $46.5B to $125.1B, and Meta's from $58.7B to $80.9B. The Economist ("The AI industry's gigantic debts are making investors nervous", 18 October 2026) reports that Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta borrowed $214B in 2026, against $83B in all of 2025, and that JPMorgan expects about $5.5T of capex through 2030, three quarters of it funded with debt.
  • Expensive debt at the GPU clouds. CoreWeave's recourse debt rose from $20.7B to $31.4B, and its quarterly interest expense of $640M was about 13 times its operating loss. Nebius roughly doubled its long-term debt to $8.5B and in October signed a $3.5B bridge loan with Citibank that matures in a year.
  • Leases that have not started. Signed leases, mostly for data centers, that have not yet begun: Microsoft $364.6B, Meta $355.7B, Oracle $288B, Amazon $164.9B and Alphabet $130.5B. That is more than $1.3T together, none of it yet on the balance sheet.
  • Partners. Meta's Hyperion campus in Louisiana sits in a joint venture of about $27B, 80% funded by Blue Owl. Meta reports a maximum exposure to loss of $68.4B across two such ventures, including $41.5B of guarantees on the value of the assets.
  • Customers and shareholders. In a single quarter Oracle took in $11.4B of customer prepayments and raised $19.9B by selling new shares.

Markets noticed. S&P Global Ratings cut Meta's credit outlook, and the Nasdaq fell 5.3% in October, its worst month since March 2025 (The New York Times). The Financial Times reported higher interest rates on new hyperscaler bonds and rising prices for insurance against default. After the 3 November midterm elections the Nasdaq fell into a correction, and one day's sell-off on 5 November erased about $2.5T of Nasdaq market value (The Wall Street Journal). The Financial Times put that day's rout at $3.2T, without naming the index.

The simple version. A family that pays for a kitchen renovation from savings can pause the work if money gets tight. A family that borrowed for it owes the monthly payment no matter what. The cloud giants are mostly the first family. Many GPU clouds are the second.

For engineers and investors: what the capex numbers include
  • Capex here is cash purchases of property and equipment from each company's cash flow statement, read from SEC XBRL data and checked against the earnings releases. All six matched.
  • It leaves out assets acquired through finance leases, which matter for Microsoft and Meta, and the leases not yet started listed above.
  • Oracle's quarters end a month earlier (August, November, February, May). CoreWeave reports its third quarter after this page's date.
  • Free cash flow definitions differ: Amazon nets out proceeds and incentives, Meta also subtracts finance lease principal, and Oracle subtracts capex as reported. Compare directions, not decimals.

Who keeps the profit

The answer: the core of the market is an oligopoly with strong margins, and its edge is a commodity with thin ones. The fattest margins in the AI build-out sit upstream, with the companies that make the chips.

Follow the money from chip to customer. The measure is operating margin, the share of revenue left after running costs and depreciation, for the latest quarter (unaudited):

  • Chips. NVIDIA kept 66.2% of its revenue as operating profit, with a gross margin of 75.0%. TSMC, which manufactures the chips, kept 58.7%.
  • Power and cooling equipment. Vertiv 22.8%.
  • Buildings. Equinix 23.3% and Digital Realty 15.9%.
  • Full-service clouds. AWS 38.7% and Google Cloud 36.2%.
  • GPU clouds. CoreWeave -1.9% and Nebius -30.2%.

Fast growth does not buy margin. Nebius grew 454% and lost 30 cents on every dollar of revenue at the operating line. Google Cloud more than doubled and kept 36 cents.

Porter's five forces, a standard way to judge how much profit an industry can keep, explains why:

  • Rivalry is high. Three giants hold 61% and compete on AI capacity, price and chips, while Oracle and the neoclouds add supply.
  • Suppliers hold the most power. NVIDIA keeps 75% gross margins and had $279B of supply and capacity commitments at the end of July. Turbines and grid connections are scarce too, as the costs section showed.
  • Buyers are concentrated. A few AI labs sign the biggest contracts and can play providers against each other.
  • Entry is split in two. A GPU cloud can start quickly with borrowed money. A full-service cloud took the leaders about two decades and hundreds of billions of dollars to build.
  • Substitutes are modest. Companies can build their own data centers, design their own chips or run smaller open models, but none of these replaces the cloud for most customers.

7 Powers, a framework for lasting advantage, adds the other half. The giants have scale economies (buying power and fleets kept busy by millions of customers), switching costs (once a company's data and applications live in one cloud, moving them is slow and expensive) and cornered resources: powered land, chip allocations and their own chips, such as Google's TPUs and Amazon's Trainium. The neoclouds win on counter-positioning, being faster and focused only on GPUs, an edge that could fade as the giants catch up.

So renting raw GPUs has so far been a thin-margin, capital-hungry business. The giants also sell the layers above it, which is the most likely reason their cloud margins are so much higher. The Wall Street Journal makes a related point: a slower build-out would give existing powered data centers scarcity value, which favors the largest players.

The simple version. In a gold rush, the people selling shovels often do better than most miners. Here the shovel sellers are the chip makers. The big clouds are miners who also own the general store and the hotel, so they earn several ways. A GPU cloud is a miner with one claim and a large loan.

What people are saying

The answer: the public conversation agrees on the facts and splits on what they mean. Everyone sees record growth and record spending. The argument is about whether the revenue will arrive in time, and who holds the bill if it does not.

The newspapers. I read The Wall Street Journal, The New York Times, the Financial Times and The Economist in full. Four themes ran through them:

  1. Cash is being burned to build. Writing in the Financial Times, Martin Wolf notes that the largest hyperscalers' forecast spending now exceeds their operating cash flow, and that financing is moving to developers, property trusts, infrastructure funds and private credit, where risks are harder to see.
  2. Debt and credit stress, covered in the build-out section above: record bond issuance, a cut to Meta's credit outlook and costlier insurance against default.
  3. The revenue gap. The Economist ("AI revenues are growing fast, but not fast enough", 28 July 2026) puts today's AI revenue at $150B to $220B a year, against roughly $2.5T a year needed to cover the capex. Goldman Sachs estimates that about $1T of revenue a year would be needed for a 10% to 20% return, The Economist reported ("The AI boom is threatened by an epidemic of unreliable numbers", 14 October 2026).
  4. The backlash. In the 3 November elections, voters chose to limit or block data centers in 40 of 41 local ballot measures (The New York Times, "A target on industry as power balance shifts", 6 November 2026, read through a university library). Texas, Georgia and Ohio hold more than 40% of planned US capacity, per Evercore ISI, and Goldman Sachs puts AI investment at 1.9% of US GDP this year (The Wall Street Journal). OpenAI is weighing whether to reset next year's capacity plans, the Journal reported on 5 November.

X. About 40 posts across seven searches, read but not engaged with. The loudest themes were spending versus revenue; a bullish reply that the sell-off misreads a boom in which demand still exceeds supply; a bubble story of debt, circular deals and falling stocks; backlog concentrated in OpenAI and Anthropic; Oracle as the most leveraged bet; and the fight over how long GPUs really last. Across posts about the group, stances split almost evenly: 6 bullish, 6 bearish and 9 mixed.

Hacker News, where engineers gather. The most-discussed thread compared the build to the railroads, and drew the reply that GPUs wear out in years while railways last generations. Another debated circular financing, in which NVIDIA invests in GPU clouds that then buy NVIDIA chips. Others argued that cheap open-weight models could squeeze the AI labs, while the clouds collect rent whichever lab wins. The most-upvoted cloud threads were about trust rather than spending: price rises, billing errors and resilience.

StockTwits, where retail traders post. In a rough count of tagged messages on 7 November, bullish outnumbered bearish for Amazon (8 to 2), Alphabet (7 to 2) and Nebius (13 to 2), while Microsoft, Meta and CoreWeave were evenly split. Most messages carry no tag, so treat this as a temperature reading, not a poll.

Reddit blocked automated reading, so I have not quoted it.

What I take from it: the crowd is right that spending is outrunning cash, and right to worry about concentration. It is split on chip lives, as the filings are. And it seems to give too little weight to how much revenue is already contracted, which buys the giants time.

The simple version. It sounds like a crowded sports bar after a wild game: everyone agrees on the score, and nobody agrees on what it means for next season.

What happens next

The answer: whether the build-out pays comes down to two questions that nobody can answer yet. Will AI revenue keep pace with the capacity being built? And will chips stay useful long enough, with money cheap enough, to earn back their cost? Crossing the two gives four futures.

  • The new utility (revenue keeps pace; long lives and cheap money). Backlog would convert on time, capex would stay high but earn its keep, and margins would hold near today's.
  • The margin squeeze (revenue keeps pace; short lives and costly money). Demand would be real, but faster chip cycles and higher rates would eat the profit. The cash-rich giants would likely gain share.
  • Digestion (revenue slows; long lives and cheap money). There would be too much capacity for a while: prices would fall, capex would be cut and customers would win, as with fiber-optic networks after 2001.
  • The bust (revenue slows; short lives and costly money). Write-downs and defaults would hit debt-funded builders. The giants would lean on their other businesses.

The gap to close is large. About $1T a year of revenue would earn a 10% to 20% return on this cycle, by Goldman Sachs's estimate, against $150B to $220B of AI revenue today (both via The Economist). My own rough estimate of AI-heavy computing sold today, built from company disclosures, is about $104.9B a year (see the one-pager below).

Signposts, in the order I would check them each quarter:

  1. Backlog turning into revenue on schedule. Microsoft says about 30% of its $683B (unaudited) lands within 12 months. If revenue growth slows while backlog keeps growing, delivery is slipping.
  2. GPU rental prices. On-demand H100s at $4.20 an hour and one-year contracts at $2.35 are today's markers. Falling prices would point to digestion.
  3. Useful-life changes. A company that shortens its chip lives is saying chips age faster than planned, as Amazon did for some servers in 2025.
  4. Credit. Bond spreads, default-swap prices and whether GPU clouds can refinance. Nebius's $3.5B bridge loan comes due in October 2027.
  5. Power and permits. Local votes, grid auctions and turbine queues decide how fast capacity can come online.

My read: the three giants sit between the new utility and the margin squeeze, because their other businesses and their contracted backlog buy them time. The debt-funded GPU clouds sit between the squeeze and digestion, where a year of slower demand or higher rates would hurt most. The suppliers of chips, power and cooling earn the highest margins while the build runs. In digestion their orders would fall first, but their margins give them a cushion that leveraged builders lack.

What it means:

  • For investors: on today's numbers, the toll collectors (chips, power and cooling) and the cash-rich giants look better placed than leveraged builders. Watch the signposts, not the headlines.
  • For operators and builders: keeping the existing fleet powered, cooled and maintained is needed in every scenario. New construction would slow in digestion and largely stop in a bust.
  • For engineers: efficiency per watt is the competitive lever: Google's PUE of 1.09 against an industry 1.54, custom chips, and software that keeps every GPU busy.

The simple version. Nobody knows which of the four boxes we will land in. A good list of things to watch is more useful than a confident forecast.

The one-pager

If the industry had to pitch itself to Y Combinator on a single page, it might read like this. The numbers are the ones above, and company figures from quarterly filings are unaudited; the framing is mine.

What it is

Renting computing power by the hour or by contract, at planet scale. Land, power, buildings, chips, network and software run as one machine, and sold to everyone from a streaming service to an AI lab.

Why an investor should care

  • A $558B market, with its latest quarter up 55% on a year earlier.
  • About $2.49T of signed backlog at the four largest players, not all of it cloud.
  • More than $1T of data center capex worldwide expected in 2026, and more than $3T a year by 2030, on Dell'Oro's forecast.
  • AWS and Google Cloud already earn operating margins of 36% to 39%.

How it makes money

Revenue = capacity online x how busy it is x price per unit x mix. Raw GPU time is close to a commodity. Databases, AI services and software on top appear to be where the margin is, and multi-year contracts give years of visibility.

How much capex it takes

About $52.6B per gigawatt in the illustration: $17.6B of building, power and cooling, and $35B of chips and servers. Jensen Huang's reported estimate is $50B to $60B. In the illustration one gigawatt holds about 600,000 GPUs, and payback would take 4.3 years at strong prices or 8.2 at today's GPU-cloud revenue per gigawatt. Companies depreciate chips over five or six years, so if chips are replaced on that cycle, the spending never really stops.

Specialized skills

Power and grid interconnection; electrical and mechanical construction; chip and system design; distributed systems and reliability engineering; capital markets, for billions in bonds, leases and joint ventures; and permitting and community relations, which the 2026 local votes made more important.

How competitive it is

Three giants hold 61%. Oracle and the neoclouds grow fastest, much of it on contracts with AI labs. Suppliers hold the pricing power: NVIDIA's operating margin was 66% in its latest quarter. A new full-service cloud would take decades and hundreds of billions; a new GPU cloud is possible, but so far it has been a thin-margin, debt-funded business.

TAM, SAM and SOM

  • TAM $558B: worldwide cloud infrastructure services over the last 12 months (Synergy).
  • SAM about $104.9B: AI-heavy computing sold by the hour or by contract, added up from company run rates. It is rough: the run rates come from different months and definitions, and Google, which does not disclose its AI revenue, is left out.
  • SOM $5.2B to $10.5B: what a well-funded new entrant could win in about five years, 5% to 10% of today's SAM. CoreWeave is the reality check: revenue running at about $10.3B a year, on 1.5 GW of active power and $31.4B of debt.

Risks, and what would change my mind

Revenue concentrated in a few AI labs; chips aging faster than their accounting lives; rising interest costs; local opposition to new data centers. I would turn more cautious if backlog stopped turning into revenue on schedule, if GPU rental prices fell, or if the GPU clouds struggled to refinance.

The pitch in one sentence: for now the world wants to rent more computing power than the industry can switch on, the giants with large profitable businesses underneath can afford to keep building, and for everyone else the question is whether revenue per gigawatt outruns depreciation and interest.

Glossary

  • 10-Q: The quarterly report a US public company files with the SEC, the market regulator. Its figures are unaudited; the annual version is the 10-K.
  • Backlog (remaining performance obligations): Revenue a company has signed contracts for but not yet delivered. Accountants call it remaining performance obligations; Microsoft reported $683B at 30 September 2026 (10-Q, unaudited).
  • Capex: Capital expenditure, money spent on things that last for years, such as buildings, power equipment and chips, rather than on day-to-day running costs. Amazon's purchases of property and equipment were $77.3B in Q3 2026 (10-Q, unaudited).
  • Credit default swap: A contract in which one side pays a fee and is paid if a borrower fails to repay its debt. A higher price means investors see more risk; the Financial Times reported on 3 November 2026 that these prices for AI borrowers had risen (FT).
  • Depreciation: Spreading the cost of an asset over the years it is expected to last, instead of counting it all at once. It is a yearly expense that involves no cash leaving that year; Microsoft recorded $11.1B of property and equipment depreciation in its fiscal Q1 2027 (unaudited).
  • EBITDA: Earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization, a rough measure of operating profit before the cost of borrowing and of aging assets. Many companies publish adjusted versions that they define themselves, so compare with care.
  • Finance lease: A lease that works like buying with installments. The user records the asset and a matching debt on its books; some companies count the payments in their capex.
  • Free cash flow: Cash from operations minus spending on capex, or what is left for debt and owners. Amazon's was negative $42.4B over the 12 months to 30 September 2026 on its own definition (unaudited).
  • GPU: Graphics processing unit, a chip that does huge numbers of sums at the same time. It began in video graphics and now does most of the heavy work in AI.
  • GPU-hour: One GPU rented for one hour, the usual price unit for AI computing. The median on-demand price for an NVIDIA H100 was $4.20 on 7 October 2026 (Uvik).
  • Hyperscaler: A company that runs data centers at very large scale and rents out the computing inside them. The three giants, Amazon, Microsoft and Google, held 61% of cloud infrastructure in Q3 2026 (Synergy Research Group).
  • Inference: Using a trained AI model to answer a question or produce a result. Each answer is small, but the total grows with every user.
  • Investment grade: A credit rating in the upper bands, meaning the rating agency judges that default is unlikely. Borrowing is usually cheaper at these ratings.
  • Joint venture: A shared business that two or more companies set up and fund together. Meta and Blue Owl formed one of about $27B for the Hyperion campus, with Blue Owl funding 80% (Hubbis); Meta says it does not consolidate two data center ventures in which it holds 20%.
  • MW and GW: Units of power. A megawatt (MW) is a million watts and a gigawatt (GW) is 1,000 MW; CoreWeave reported 1.5 GW of active power at 30 June 2026 (unaudited).
  • Neocloud: A newer company built mainly to rent out GPU computing for AI, such as CoreWeave and Nebius. Most lack the older businesses that fund the giants' building.
  • Operating margin: Operating profit divided by revenue, or how many cents of each sales dollar is left after running costs and depreciation. AWS's was 38.7% in Q3 2026 (Amazon release, unaudited).
  • PUE: Power usage effectiveness, total power drawn by a data center divided by the power reaching its computers. A perfect score is 1.0; Google reports 1.09 against an industry average of 1.54 (Google).
  • SAM: Serviceable addressable market, the part of the total market a business could actually serve with what it sells. This page estimates one for AI-heavy computing.
  • SOM: Serviceable obtainable market, the slice of the SAM a business could realistically win. For a new entrant, it is a share of today's SAM over a few years; the page treats it as an estimate, not a forecast.
  • Spot capacity: Spare cloud machines sold at a deep discount, up to 90% off on AWS, with the catch that the provider can take them back at short notice (AWS).
  • TAM: Total addressable market, the whole yearly revenue available in a market. Here it is cloud infrastructure services: $558B over the 12 months to Q3 2026 (Synergy Research Group).
  • Token: A small chunk of text, roughly a word or part of one, that AI models read and write. Many AI services charge per million tokens.
  • Training: Teaching an AI model by running it over very large amounts of data. It can take thousands of chips for weeks, then the finished model moves on to inference.
  • Useful life: The number of years a company assumes an asset will last, which sets how fast it is depreciated. It is a judgment; Meta extended most servers to 5.5 years from 1 January 2025, which cut its 2025 depreciation by $2.92B (10-K).
  • Utilization: The share of capacity actually in use. A data center full of idle chips earns little, so keeping utilization high matters.
